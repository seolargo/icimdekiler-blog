## Introduction: A Small Genre

When people hear "classical music," most think of big things: symphonies, operas, orchestras of a hundred players. Yet one of the most intimate genres of classical music is very small. A singer, a piano and a few minutes. The Germans call it a Lied; the plural is Lieder.

The Lied is an art song born in the nineteenth-century German-speaking world. It looks as simple as a folk song, but behind it stand great poets and great composers. Most last between two and five minutes. In that short time a person's whole inner world can be told.

Listening to this genre, you feel you are sitting not in a concert hall but in someone's room. Because that is how the Lied was born: not for great stages, but for small rooms.

## The Equality of Poetry and Music

The first thing that sets the Lied apart from an ordinary song is its words. The words are not lines written for a song, but real poems. They are taken from the books of poets such as Goethe, Eichendorff, Heine and Wilhelm Müller.

That is why in the Lied words and music are equals. The music is written not to decorate the poem but to understand it. The composer reads the poem, finds the feeling inside it and turns it into sound. When a word changes, the melody changes too. When a line turns dark, the key darkens with it.

In Der Wanderer, as the traveller describes the country of his dreams, the music suddenly moves into major. In Mondnacht, as the soul spreads its wings, the melody rises for the first time. These are not ornaments. They are the poem itself, spoken this time in sound.

That is why, to really understand a Lied, you need to know its words. Even for a listener who knows no German, reading beforehand what the poem says changes the listening completely.

## What the Piano Tells

In the Lied the piano does not merely accompany. It is part of the story. While the singer sings the words, the piano often sets the scene: the air, the place, the time.

The heavy, numb chords in Der Wanderer are the traveller's tired footsteps. In Frühlingstraum, the sharp strokes that suddenly cut off the sweet dream melody are the crowing of cocks and the cries of ravens. In Mondnacht, the chords trembling in the high register are moonlight. In Nacht und Träume, the ceaselessly rippling lower line is the night itself.

Sometimes the piano says what the singer cannot. The song ends, the voice falls silent, but the piano goes on a little longer. At the end of Mondnacht the soul keeps flying, though we can no longer see it; it is the piano, not the words, that tells us this.

That is why in the Lied the pianist is not someone standing behind the singer but their partner. Together the two tell a single story.

## Salons, Cycles and Masters

The Lied was born not in great concert halls but in homes. In the early nineteenth century the piano was beginning to enter middle-class homes. In Vienna, Schubert's friends gathered in houses to hear his songs. These small evenings later came to be called Schubertiades. The Lied's intimate air comes from those rooms.

Schubert, considered the father of the genre, wrote more than six hundred Lieder in his short life. Gretchen am Spinnrade, a scene from Goethe's Faust that he composed at just seventeen, is often named as the moment the genre was born.

Some Lieder stand alone. Others come together to tell a story. This is called a song cycle, in German a Liederzyklus. Schubert's Winterreise is the twenty-four-song winter journey of an abandoned man. Schumann's Dichterliebe and Liederkreis are also among the best-known examples of the form. A cycle is listened to like the chapters of a novel.

After Schubert the genre grew in the hands of masters very different from one another. Schumann made it more poetic and more personal. Brahms returned to the simplicity of folk song. Hugo Wolf worked out every word with music, one by one. Mahler and Richard Strauss carried the Lied into the orchestra. But the essence of the genre always stayed the same: to sing a person's inner voice, in a few minutes, through a poem.

## Two Names on Record

In the twentieth century the most famous voice of this genre was Dietrich Fischer-Dieskau. The German baritone recorded almost all of the songs Schubert wrote for the male voice. The strength of his interpretation lies in his not shouting. He sings the songs not as a show but as an inner conversation. He gives each word together with its meaning.

The pianist beside him, Gerald Moore, is the one who showed everyone how important the pianist is in the Lied. With him the accompanist stopped being someone in the singer's shadow and became a true partner. The recordings the two made together are still considered the gateway to the genre.

## Closing

The Lied is a small genre, but its smallness is not a shortcoming. A voice and a piano can tell what a great orchestra cannot: a traveller's weariness, a girl's silent longing, the pain of waking from a dream.

Listening to these songs, you are not watching a concert. Someone is telling you, in a hushed voice, what is inside them. The whole secret of the Lied lies in that closeness.
