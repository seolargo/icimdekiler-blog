## Introduction: A Flower and a Word

Anyone who listens to the songs of the German Romantics notices the same thing: these people are always longing for something. Sometimes a country, sometimes a lover, sometimes a dream. But they never arrive at what they long for.

This feeling has a symbol: the blue flower. In his novel Heinrich von Ofterdingen, Novalis tells of a blue flower a young man sees in a dream. The man can never forget that flower and spends his life searching for it. The blue flower becomes the name for longing for the unreachable.

German also has a word for this feeling: Sehnsucht. It does not translate fully into Turkish. It resembles "hasret," but hasret is for something you know. Sehnsucht, more often, is felt for something you have never seen, something that may not even exist.

This essay looks at where that longing came from, and at how it became sound in a few of Schubert's songs.

## Where This Longing Comes From

The first answer that comes to mind is religion. That is true, but incomplete. The German longing was born not from a single source but from several causes layered on one another.

The religious side is Pietism. This movement, strong in eighteenth-century Germany, valued inner feeling more than religious rules. The world was a transient place; the true homeland lay in the world beyond. Many Romantics, such as Novalis and Schleiermacher, grew up in such families. The feeling "my true home is not here" came from this. Then, in the Romantics' hands, it separated from religion and became a feeling in its own right.

The political side was more concrete. At the time there was no state called "Germany." There were hundreds of principalities, duchies and city-states. There was a shared language and culture, but no homeland. People longed for a whole they could belong to. Beneath the admiration for the Middle Ages lay the dream that "once we were whole."

The educated middle class had almost no voice in politics. Those who could not change the outer world turned inward: to feeling, poetry, music, philosophy. In German this is called Innerlichkeit, inwardness.

Then there was the age itself. The Enlightenment had begun to explain everything through reason, and science and industry to make the world measurable. The Romantics felt this left the world disenchanted and cold. They searched for the lost magic, the infinite, the mystery.

Philosophy, too, gave this search a ground. Kant had said that we can never fully know the essence of things. The Romantics drew from this the conclusion that the true reality always lies somewhere we cannot reach. That is the philosophical foundation of the idea of the blue flower.

Finally, geography. A cold, grey northern country with long winters. The longing for the sun, for the south, for Italy comes partly from here.

This feeling does not belong to the Germans alone. We find its like in the English Romantics, in the Portuguese saudade, in our own hasret and hüzün. But the Germans turned it into a worldview, an artistic programme, and gave it a name. That is why it stands out so much in them.

## The Place Where You Are Not: Der Wanderer

The clearest form of this longing is in Schubert's song Der Wanderer (D 489). The words come from an 1808 poem by Georg Philipp Schmidt von Lübeck. Schubert set it in 1816.

A traveller comes down from the mountains at dusk. The valley is full of mist, the sea roars in the distance. He walks in silence and keeps asking himself the same question: "Where to?" The place he is in never does him good. Even the sun is cold here. The flowers look withered, life looks old. People's words are an empty hum. Wherever he goes, he is a stranger.

Then his own country comes to mind. But it is a strange country. A place he has always sought, whose existence he senses within, but has never seen. It is as green as hope. Its roses bloom there, his friends walk there, his dead loved ones rise again there. Everyone speaks his language. "Where are you?" he calls.

The answer comes as a ghostly whisper: "Dort, wo du nicht bist, dort ist das Glück." Where you are not, there is happiness.

This sentence is the exact description of the blue flower. The place sought seems truly to exist, but by its very definition it cannot be reached. Wherever the traveller arrives, that place stops being that country.

The music follows this story step by step. The song opens in a dark, heavy minor key; the voice almost speaks. At "the sun seems so cold to me here," the piano repeats slow, numb chords one after another. Schubert valued this melody so much that he built the slow movement of his famous piano work, the Wanderer-Fantasie (D 760), on it. As the imagined country is described, the key suddenly turns to major, light comes in, the tempo quickens. Then the music falls back into darkness. That last whisper is repeated, and the song closes on a low, tired voice.

In the recording by Fischer-Dieskau and Gerald Moore, that last sentence sinks almost to a whisper. The ghostly voice really seems to come from a ghost.

## The Unknown Country: Mignon and Italy

The same wound opens in Mignon, in Goethe's novel Wilhelm Meister's Apprenticeship. Mignon is a mysterious young girl. Born in Italy, kidnapped as a child, she grew up in a troupe of acrobats. She does not quite know who she is. She always longs for a faraway place and a faraway someone.

Schubert set Mignon's song, the poem Nur wer die Sehnsucht kennt, no fewer than six times. The best known is the last song of the 1826 set of four (D 877). The poem is short: only one who knows longing knows what I suffer. I am all alone, torn from every joy. I look at the sky, toward that distant side. The one who loves and knows me is very far away. My head spins, I burn inside. And the first line returns.

The poem draws a circle. The longing is not resolved; it comes back to the start. So does the music. It begins calm and low, rises and tightens once at "my head spins, I burn inside," then at once draws back. The first line returns with the same calm. As if nothing had happened. But the pain is still there.

Mignon's other famous song in the novel, Kennst du das Land, names the longed-for place openly: Italy. Lemon trees, golden oranges glowing among dark leaves, myrtle and laurel, a house with marble statues, mules on a misty mountain path. Mignon describes these not like a memory but like a dream. Because she was torn from there as a child.

Why Italy? The novel's answer is simple: it is the homeland Mignon lost. But behind it lies Goethe's own longing too. His father had gone to Italy when young, and Roman engravings hung in their home. Goethe wrote this poem in the early 1780s, without ever having seen Italy. In 1786 he slipped away from his life in Weimar without telling anyone and went to Italy, where he stayed two years. He later described that journey as his second birth.

Seen more broadly, Italy was the dream of a whole culture. For German intellectuals it was the exact opposite of the cold, grey North: sun, warmth, ancient Rome, art. Winckelmann had exalted ancient art, and young nobles flocked to Italy on the Grand Tour.

So here Italy is less a country than a symbol. It is the same thing as Der Wanderer's "country as green as hope." The only difference is this: Goethe went and saw it in the end. Mignon can never return, and in the novel she dies inside her longing.

## Flowers of Ice: Frühlingstraum

Longing is not always directed at a faraway country. Sometimes the place one escapes to is a dream. For the Romantics, the day, that is, real life, was ordinary and cold. The truly deep world lay in the night and in dreams. It is no accident that the blue flower first appears in a dream.

Frühlingstraum (Dream of Spring), the eleventh song of Schubert's cycle Winterreise, tells the most painful form of this dream. The words are by Wilhelm Müller. Schubert set it in 1827, a year before his death. In Winterreise, a young man whose beloved has become engaged to someone else leaves the town by night in the depths of winter. He walks aimlessly along snowy roads and grows a little lonelier with every song.

In the middle of the journey he sleeps in a village house and dreams of spring. Colourful flowers, green meadows, birdsong. The cocks crow and he wakes. It is cold and dark, and ravens shriek on the roof. Then he looks at the window: there are leaves on the glass. Who painted them?

Those patterns on the glass are ice. In German they are called Eisblumen, ice flowers. In the dream there were real flowers; on waking, what he is left with is flowers of ice. The whole pain of the song lies between these two flowers.

He sleeps again and this time sees love: a beautiful girl, kisses, happiness. The cocks crow again, and his heart wakes again. He sits alone and closes his eyes. His heart is still beating warmly. He asks: when will the leaves on the window turn green? When will I hold my beloved in my arms? No answer comes.

The music makes us live this cycle twice. First a light, swaying, waltz-like, sweet dream melody. Then suddenly a harsh, fast awakening; sharp strokes in the piano like the cries of cocks and ravens. Then a slow, still, hesitant passage of reflection. Then everything starts again from the beginning. The song ends on an open wound.

In another song, Nacht und Träume, the dream was a sacred refuge: "Come back, holy night." Here the dream turns into a torment that reminds one how cold real life is. Here the blue flower has become an ice flower.

## Conclusion: The Place Never Reached

Three songs, three different faces. The traveller searches for a country he has never seen. Mignon longs for a country she was torn from as a child. The winter traveller longs for a spring he saw in a dream. But they share the same thing: what is longed for either never existed, or does not return, or turns to ice on waking.

That is why the Romantics' longing is not a lack but something like a state of being. Finding the blue flower would mean losing it. The real point is not arriving but being on the road.

Schubert says this better than words. In his songs light always enters for a moment, the major key gives hope for a moment. Then the music returns to darkness. But that moment of light stays inside the listener. Perhaps that is exactly what the blue flower is: something never grasped, but never forgotten once seen.
