baslik: Two Pillars
---
People look for someone like themselves. They do it without knowing. In a crowded room their eye catches on someone who laughs at the same joke, loves the same silence, looks at the same star. "Opposites attract," they say. Perhaps for an evening they do. But for a lifetime a person wants someone who speaks their own language. Someone who stands on the same values, looks in the same direction, can sit at the same table.

Still, difference is not shut out entirely. One talks, the other listens. One draws the road, the other lingers along it. The shy one admires the bold, the bold one rests in the shy one's gentleness. When the roots are in the same soil, the branches can reach in different directions. But when the roots are in different soils, even the loveliest flower fades after a season.

And there is familiarity disguised as likeness. Sometimes we are drawn to someone because "they are so like me." Yet they are like not us but our past. They recall the tone of a voice we heard in childhood, a distance we saw, a love we waited for that never came. The heart recognises them and says, "There." But recognising is not always the same as being good for us. Some familiar streets lead back to old wounds.

How do we know we are drawn to the wrong person? Most often the body tells us, and the mind catches up later. If there is more anxiety than peace. If a storm breaks inside us every time the phone stays silent. If one day we rise to the skies and the next fall to the ground, and call that wave love. If beside them we weigh our words, make ourselves small, give ourselves up so as not to anger them. If their words are lovely but their hands are empty. If we are always the one giving and they always the one taking. And most of all, if we love not them but who they might become. Every sentence that begins "If only they would change" is really saying that who they are today is not enough for us.

The right person usually arrives quietly. Not like thunder, but like the smell of earth after rain. Beside them your breathing slows. Their words and their actions stand in the same place. Their attention is not there one day and gone the next; it is like a steady light. On your hard day they are at your door. And most important, beside them you do not shrink, you grow. The wrong person feels intense but restless. The right person calm but safe. Perhaps when we measure love we should look not at how fast the heart beats, but at how easily.

So what are we looking for? A life in which I am at the centre and they stand beside me. Psychology names this in several ways. Murray Bowen calls it differentiation of self: being able to be very close to someone without being lost in them. Some call it interdependence: neither being unable to live without them nor needing no one at all. Two people, each on their own feet, yet leaning on each other. Kahlil Gibran describes it with a temple. Lovers should be like two pillars holding up the same roof. Side by side, but not in each other's shadow. Because if the pillars stand too close, the roof falls.

And yet there is another voice inside us. "Let me make them my centre," it says. "Let me give them all my time, all my love. Let their happiness be my work, their troubles my troubles." This voice is very tempting. Songs are written in this voice, poems are sung in it. "I cannot live without you" is offered as the highest form of love. Putting someone at the centre gives life a direction. It is easier to look at them than to face our own emptiness. And deepest of all there is a very old longing: to become one with someone, for loneliness at last to end.

But this voice has a root, and that root is familiar. It feeds from the same soil as the wish to rescue. The heart drawn to someone troubled, wounded, broken, wanting to mend them, is really searching for its own worth. "If I am useful, if I am needed, I am worth something," it says. Rescuing someone is one branch of this thought. Devoting oneself entirely to someone is its farthest branch. One helps, the other gives their whole self. But both look for their worth not in themselves but in what they give. The root is the same: to feel valuable, to be needed, not to be left.

And sometimes, without ever noticing, we try to make the other person dependent on us. Not out of ill will. Not by making a plan. Simply by doing for them the things they could do themselves. By finding a remedy for every trouble before they have even thought about it. By feeling an indescribable unease inside as they grow stronger. Because in a corner of the heart this sentence is written: "If they need me, they won't leave me." This is not a wish for control but a fear. A small shelter built against the fear of loss.

This fear does not make us a bad person. It is born of love and of fear. Perhaps it is left over from days in childhood when we had to look after someone, calm the air at home, carry the grown-ups' burden too early. But as we grow there is something we need to learn: need ends one day. The wound heals, the break mends, a person learns to stand on their own feet. If the bond was woven only of need, that day the thread snaps too.

There is a way to tell. When the person you love grows stronger, when they can walk their own road, look at what is inside you. If there is joy and relief, your love is standing in the right place. If there is an emptiness, a feeling of "I'm no use anymore," the old root has put out a shoot again.

In healthy love the aim is not for someone to need you. It is for them to choose you. Need is seasonal; it comes and it goes. But a choice can be made anew every morning. Every winter, every spring, every tired evening. Two pillars stand side by side. One does not carry the other, one does not stand in the other's shadow. But together, under the same roof, they carry a lifetime.
