---
baslik: What a Body Remembers
---
Now, years later, I understand that on none of those nights was I really looking only for a body, but back then believing it, telling myself that story, was far easier than carrying the weight of all those empty rooms, the phones ringing toward dawn, the old hurts I could tell no one.

I used to tell myself: I expect nothing anymore. Not to be loved, not to be missed, not even for someone to still be there beside me when I wake up in the morning. Just one night, just one skin, just those few hours of someone's breath against my neck. When I said this my voice came out firm, it came out firm even in front of the mirror, but now I know that a person uses their firmest voice to cover what they fear most.

I still remember one of those women, I will not write her name here. Perhaps what I remember is not her but the small things she left behind. A thin hair clip forgotten at the edge of the bed, dust motes hanging in the pale light coming through the window in the morning, the way, after putting two sugars in her coffee, she tapped the spoon three times against the rim of the glass, and I never asked why she did that, had I asked I might have heard the story of a mother, a kitchen, a distant childhood, and that would have been exactly the thing I was running from.

Because when a person thinks they are touching a body, they are really touching that body's whole past. I did not know this then. Or I knew it and chose not to, and only now, writing these lines, at my desk at midnight, listening to the last bus passing in the street, can I tell the difference between the two.

On those nights I often thought: in this city, right now, how many people like me have put their hearts away on the top shelf of a cupboard? How many women, how many men, tired of being loved or carrying the quiet shame of never having been loved, will lie down beside someone and then quietly put their shoes back on? And this thought gave me a strange comfort, knowing that my loneliness belonged to a crowd, like that familiar sadness I feel on a ferry on a rainy evening, looking at the faces of everyone lost in their own thoughts.

But sadness, however familiar, does not protect you. One morning, while that woman whose name I will not write was sleeping, I looked at her face and a sentence passed through me that I never wanted, never gave permission to: I wish she wouldn't go. In that moment I felt the whole story I had been telling myself, that firm voice, all the promises I had made in front of the mirror, crack between my fingers like a thin porcelain cup. I was afraid. Truly afraid. Because the thing I had run from for years had come back, at the moment I least expected, from behind a hair clip, from behind the sound of a spoon.

She left, of course. And I did not go after her. Perhaps that was right, perhaps it was not, I still do not know.

But this I know: seeing someone only as a body is one of the most convincing lies a person can tell themselves. Some live their whole lives with this lie, and perhaps for them it is not a lie, perhaps it really is enough for them, it is not for me to judge. But for me, in that room, in that pale morning light, learning that a body never really comes alone, that it always carries with it a story, a childhood, a fear and a small, shy hope, was the quietest and deepest lesson of my life.

The hair clip is still in my drawer. I know now why I have not thrown it away, and why I am writing these lines.
