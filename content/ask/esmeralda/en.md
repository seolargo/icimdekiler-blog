baslik: Esmeralda
---
At midnight I listened to a song. It was in French and I did not understand most of the words. Three men took turns singing the same woman's name. Belle, they said. Beautiful.

Then I read her story. The song came from Victor Hugo's novel Notre-Dame de Paris. And that woman, Esmeralda, has been living the same thing for two hundred years.

A sixteen-year-old gypsy girl dancing in the streets of Paris. A little goat at her side, a kind of freedom in her feet. Everyone who sees her stops and looks. They look, but they do not see.

Three men fall in love with her. Quasimodo, the cathedral's hunchbacked bell-ringer, sees in her the first person in his life who has shown him tenderness. The priest Frollo sees the devil in her and lays the sin of his own desire on her shoulders. Captain Phoebus sees nothing more than a passing fancy for one night. All three, looking at Esmeralda, are really looking at themselves.

When I listened to the song again I noticed it: Esmeralda does not speak even once. Everyone tells her story. She is only told.

After writing those two sentences I stayed with them for a while. The same words, the same woman, the same action. Only their places have changed. In the first, there are the ones who tell, and Esmeralda is an object inside their sentence. In the second, the sentence begins with her. For a moment the reader waits, thinking now she will speak. But the verb arrives and the expectation breaks: she is told. She has become the owner of the sentence, and still she can do nothing. She is not someone who acts, but someone acted upon. The "only" that slips in between closes the door. There is nothing else. She does not speak, she does not decide. Her existence is nothing but other people's words. In rhetoric, when words come back in the second sentence with their places swapped, it is called chiasmus, a crossed structure. Perhaps this is not a full cross, but it plays the same trick. The words stay the same; only whose hands they are in changes. You feel the difference without thinking about it. Esmeralda's story is a little like that. Everything turns around her, yet nothing is in her hands.

In psychology this is called projection. People, it seems, mostly see others through their own needs, fears and desires. When we fall in love, we fall in love not with the person in front of us but with the picture we have built in our heads. We load onto them the qualities that will make up for what we lack. What bothers us most in someone is sometimes something we will not accept in ourselves. There is no other explanation for Frollo counting his own desire as Esmeralda's crime. We see things not as they are, but as we are.

Say someone told you that you are not handsome. That sentence says more about their eye than about your face. All it says is that you do not fit the picture in their head. And that picture was made from their past, their surroundings, the things they have watched. Maybe they were hurt that day, maybe they were unsure of themselves. Or maybe that was simply their taste, and there is nothing lacking in anyone because of it. But one person's word is never a verdict. Someone else looks at the same face and sees something entirely different.

Still, we learn who we are largely from how others look at us. As children we are told we are clever, we are told we are naughty, we are told we are shy. After a while we believe it. A person always seen through the same label slowly begins to resemble it. Beautiful, they say; sinful, they say; foreign, they say. A crime is committed and the fingers turn to her at once. Because she is a gypsy. Because she is the other. Society looks for a back to carry its own darkness, and the easiest back it finds is hers.

At one point in the story we learn that Esmeralda is not even a gypsy. She was stolen as a baby. Her real name is Agnès. For years her mother has shut herself in a cell, weeping as she clutches a tiny shoe of the daughter she lost.

One day, while fleeing, Esmeralda falls in front of that cell. The woman takes her for a gypsy and seizes her with hatred. Then a baby's shoe comes out of the little pouch around the girl's neck. The pair of the shoe hidden away for all those years. Mother and daughter find each other. For the first time Esmeralda learns who she is, whose daughter she is.

Her mother hides her in the cell and lies to the soldiers. Just as they are about to leave, Esmeralda hears Phoebus's voice outside and, unable to bear it, calls out to him. The soldiers tear her from her mother's arms and take her away.

When I read this I stayed still for a while. Is a person lost on the very day they learn who they are? And all for calling out to someone who never saw her at all.

And yet people can free themselves from the way others look at them. They can define themselves anew. It does not even take much. A few people who truly listen to them, a place where they can tell their own story, and a little time. Esmeralda had none of these. Many looked; no one listened.

When Hugo wrote this novel, Notre-Dame was an old building falling into ruin. The book was loved so much that they raised the cathedral up again. They saved the stones.

No one saved Esmeralda.

At the end of the song the three men sing the same word again. Belle. I was thinking of something else.

No one had ever asked her name.
