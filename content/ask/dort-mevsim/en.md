baslik: Four Seasons
---
Being drawn to a person can take a few seconds. A glance, a smile, a sentence is enough. The heart speeds up, the mind fixes on that person. The brain releases dopamine and noradrenaline, and sleep slips away. In this first period we do not really know the other person. We fill in what we do not know with imagination. Most of the time we love not the real person but the version in our head.

When attraction is returned, romantic love begins. Time is spent together, and "I" slowly becomes "we." The excitement is still high, but now we start to know the real person. According to research, this intense period usually lasts somewhere between six months and two or three years. Then an inevitable moment comes: the encounter with reality.

At this stage the ideal person in our head gives way to the real one. Flaws appear, differences become clear, the first serious fights happen. The excitement fades. Many relationships end right here. Because people read this calming down as "love is over." Yet it is not an ending but a transition. In couples who cross this threshold a new bond forms. Now oxytocin and vasopressin come to the fore in the brain. The racing heart is replaced by the feeling "I am at ease with you." Then comes commitment. This is no longer a feeling but a decision. Two people choose each other knowingly. As the years pass, love comes to rest on friendship, respect and a shared past. It draws its strength not from excitement but from calm and trust.

The psychologist Robert Sternberg describes love as a triangle. At one corner is passion, that is, attraction and desire. At another, intimacy, the emotional bond and sharing. At the last corner, commitment, the decision to stay together. If there is only passion, it is infatuation: it flares up quickly and goes out quickly. If there is intimacy and commitment without passion, it is companionate love. When all three come together, what Sternberg calls "consummate love" appears. But keeping this triangle standing does not happen on its own; it takes effort.

All these stages take not months but years. Nor are they separated by sharp lines. They overlap, and sometimes we go back. In relationships born from friendship, attraction and attachment arrive almost at the same time. In long-distance relationships the process stretches out, because getting to know someone inside real life is delayed.

That is why there is an old saying: to know someone, you have to spend four seasons with them. In the first months everyone shows their best side. But it is hard to keep that up for long. Within a year the mask slowly slips. At holidays and birthdays you see what they are like with their family. In a stressful period you learn whether they stay calm or turn their anger on you. When you are ill you notice whether they are by your side or vanish. When plans fall apart on a holiday, when they are tired, when you are alone together for a long time, you learn who they are. You see how they withdraw in winter and their energy in summer. And in the first big fight it becomes clear whether they can apologise, whether they take a step toward making up. Four seasons is not a scientific rule. But it reminds us that most of a life is made not of exciting moments but of ordinary and difficult days.

Of course, not everyone waits this long. Many people meet and marry quickly. In Turkey arranged introductions are still common. Because the families, values and surroundings are known in advance, the getting-to-know period is kept short. Some do not want to wait because they have reached a certain age and are ready to marry. Some do not consider a long relationship right for cultural or religious reasons. And some say, "I knew the moment I saw them." This carries a risk. A study in the United States shows that couples who were together for less than a year before marrying have a higher risk of divorce than those who were together longer. But there are plenty of couples who married quickly and are very happy. So speed alone is not decisive.

The real difference is in the order. Couples who marry quickly go through the same stages. It is just that the encounter with reality, the first fights and the four seasons come not before the marriage but after it. The things that make this period easier to get through are clear: shared values, realistic expectations, a family standing behind them, and seeing marriage not as a feeling but as work. Those who pass through this period with patience can build a strong bond over time. For those who cannot, it can be shattering.

In the end, whether the road is long or short, it leads to the same question. Attraction opens the door, love walks in, commitment builds the house. But living in that house is only possible by staying on as the seasons change. The real point is not to keep the excitement alive forever. It is being able to choose each other again, when the excitement fades too, when winter comes.
