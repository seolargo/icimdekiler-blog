baslik: The Blue Flower
---
There is a word in German: Sehnsucht. In everyday speech it means a very strong longing. When a German says to their lover "Ich habe Sehnsucht nach dir," they are saying, "I miss you so much, it burns inside me." It is close to the Turkish "hasret," but more intense. It can be felt for something known, or for something never known at all. But it shows its real power in the second: a deep longing for something not quite known, something that may never have existed.

In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries this word became almost a philosophy of life in Germany. For the Romantic poets, Sehnsucht was the deepest feeling, the one that draws a person beyond ordinary life. And the strongest symbol of this feeling blossomed in a young poet's dream.

That poet's name was Novalis. His real name was Friedrich von Hardenberg. He lived only twenty-eight years. The greatest grief of his life was the death of Sophie, to whom he had become engaged very young. Most of what he wrote was nourished by that loss, by that longing. In his unfinished novel *Heinrich von Ofterdingen*, a young man sees a blue flower in a dream. The flower is so beautiful that after he wakes he cannot get it out of his mind. He sets out to find it.

This blue flower was not a real flower. It was a symbol carrying love, poetry, the infinite and the wholeness of life all at once. It is always sought, but even when found it is never fully grasped. In time it became the emblem of all German Romanticism. "Seeking the blue flower" meant chasing an unreachable longing.

In the same years Goethe gave this feeling a voice too. In his novel *Wilhelm Meister's Apprenticeship* there is a mysterious girl named Mignon. Torn from her homeland as a child, she grew up in a wandering troupe of performers. She remembers neither her family nor her home clearly. She longs only for a sunny land of lemon trees, and for someone. Mignon sings a song:

> Only one who knows longing
> Knows what I suffer!
> Alone, torn away
> From every joy,
> I look up at the sky
> Toward that side.
> Ah! The one who loves me, who knows me
> Is far away now.
> My head spins, and burns
> Inside me, to the depths.
> Only one who knows longing
> Knows what I suffer!

The poem's power lies in what it does not say. Who is longed for, and where, is not clear. One simply looks "toward that side." Someone who loves and knows me is far away. Perhaps they really exist, perhaps they never did. And the poem says this feeling cannot be explained. Only one who has lived it can understand. For anyone who has not, it is just a word.

This poem went so deep that composers turned it into song again and again. In that era melodies for voice and piano were written on famous poems; in German this is called a Lied. Beethoven wrote four different melodies for this poem. Schubert set it six times. Tchaikovsky set it again in a Russian translation, and the song spread in English as *None but the Lonely Heart*. It even gave its name to a Hollywood film in 1944. A single poem passed from Germany to Russia, and from there to America, through different hearts over a hundred and fifty years. All of them tried to tell the same thing.

Goethe's novel also asked a question: dream or reality? Wilhelm was the son of a merchant family, but his head was in the theatre. He ran away from home and threw himself into stages, love affairs and disappointments. In the end he did not give up his dream entirely, but he learned to carry it into real life. Mignon, though, stayed inside Sehnsucht and could not get out. Her end was tragic. Novalis loved this novel at first, then grew angry with it. He could not accept practical life winning out over dreams. In a sense he wrote the blue flower as an answer to Goethe. And so the two books began to speak to each other. One said "accept reality," the other "follow the blue flower."

This argument had not ended even two hundred years later. In 1995 the English writer Penelope Fitzgerald wrote a novel called *The Blue Flower*. This time the subject was Novalis himself. She told of the young poet falling in love with Sophie, of his family, his youth. So one wrote Sehnsucht itself, the other the man who lived that Sehnsucht.

Sehnsucht did not stay in literature. In the 2000s, researchers at the Max Planck Institute in Berlin studied it scientifically and called it "life longings." They found six characteristics. First, a utopian picture of the ideal state of life. Second, the feeling that life is incomplete. Third, three times at once: it comes from the past, is felt in the present, reaches into the future. Fourth, the sweet and the bitter intertwined. Fifth, pushing a person to reflect on their own life. And sixth, symbolic richness. A person, a place, a scene becomes the symbol of something much larger. A girl who studies at Boğaziçi, a Germany to go to together, a woman's youth one never witnessed. All of them sit inside this sixth point.

The research has another important finding. Living with Sehnsucht is not bad. When people can carry this longing without drowning in it, it becomes a force that gives their life direction. But when the longing takes hold of a person and constantly makes them feel "my life is incomplete," unhappiness grows.

One of Sehnsucht's favourite places is someone newly met. You know almost nothing about someone you have just met. A face, a few sentences, a smile, perhaps a school or a city. Everything else is blank. And the mind fills those blanks with its own longings. The person stops being a person and becomes a screen. And you project your own dreams onto that screen. The psychologist Dorothy Tennov called this limerence. Thinking about someone constantly, searching every message for meaning, seeing them as flawless, building a future with them without knowing them. The feeling growing as the uncertainty grows. Limerence is like Sehnsucht dressed onto a person.

There are ways to recognise it. If you are thinking not of them but of the dream about them. With them in Germany, with them on holiday, with them in a life. If you feel a great deal despite knowing them very little. If something inside you collapses when you see a real flaw of theirs. If, when you actually spend time with them, it does not thrill you as much as it does in your head. As the real person emerges, one of two things happens. Either the dream collapses, you say "this wasn't it," and the longing moves on to someone else. Or the dream slowly fades and the real person takes its place. And sometimes that real person turns out less bright than the dream, but warmer.

So will we never reach Sehnsucht? Fully, permanently, probably not. That is its very definition. But it is not so dark. C. S. Lewis, the author of *The Chronicles of Narnia*, described this feeling throughout his life and sometimes called it joy. For him, Sehnsucht touches us now and then, for an instant. In a piece of music, a landscape, a conversation, beside someone. For a few seconds you say, "this is it." Then it passes. But that moment is real, and it comes again and again throughout life.

The longing itself gives something too. Without this longing a person would not search, travel, create or love. Most of music, poetry, travel and curiosity are born of it. Novalis's blue flower, Goethe's poem, Schubert's songs are all children of this unreachable thing. So not reaching it does not mean life is empty. It is exactly what moves you.

The problem is not failing to reach it, but making reaching it a condition. If you say, "if I don't reach it, nothing means anything," life will always taste bitter. If you say, "I touch it now and then, and walk on after it," it becomes bearable, even beautiful. Life is like a moderately spicy dish. Its sweet side is real: laughing with a friend, music you love, the first step into a new city, the feeling of finishing a piece of work, a good conversation, a good meal. These are not meadows. But they are what make the heat edible.

In the end the blue flower always blooms a little further ahead. Heinrich set out to seek it, Mignon faded away longing for it, Wilhelm learned to carry it into real life. We walk somewhere between these three. We never quite arrive. But along the way, sometimes, we come very close. And perhaps Goethe was right: only one who knows longing understands. But one who knows longing also knows why life is worth living.
