baslik: Looking in the Same Direction
---
Love brings two people side by side. But what sets them on the same road is not love; it is values. Values are the core beliefs that give a person's life its direction. Their stance on family, marriage, faith, money, freedom, where to live, whether or not to have children. These do not change easily, because they are bound up with who a person is. Trying to change someone's values is like trying to change their compass.

Not every difference is a difference in values. One loves the mountains, the other the sea. One listens to jazz, the other to rock. These are differences of taste, and they often enrich a relationship. When one goes along with the other's wish, they give up something small, and perhaps discover something new. But with a difference in values it is another matter. There, when one goes along with the other, they give up not something small but themselves. And the price of giving up yourself comes back over time as resentment.

Still, not every difference in values is a wall. Some differences can be lived with. One may be more religious, the other less. One saves, the other spends. One loves a crowd, the other their home. If both respect each other, if no one forces anyone, these differences can stand side by side. The relationship researcher John Gottman found that most of couples' arguments are never resolved. Happy couples, too, go on thinking differently about the same things for years. What makes them happy is not that they erase their differences, but that they learn to live with them.

But there are some differences that have no middle ground. A child either happens or it does not. If marriage is a condition for someone, it either happens or it does not. If one wants to put down roots here and the other wants to go far away, two roads cannot be walked at once. In these differences, when one says "all right," they are really letting go of a fundamental part of their life. Today perhaps it does not show. But five years on, ten years on, that part let go of returns as an emptiness. The sentence "I erased myself for them" is love's quietest enemy.

Take a woman, for instance. She says to the man she loves: "I want to live these feelings with my spouse. It can't be without marriage." When first heard, this sentence can sound like a condition, like a wall. But most of the time it is speaking not of a piece of paper but of what that paper means. It means, "I want to see that you have truly chosen me." It means, "This is not a game, it is a lifetime." Or it is the voice of her faith, her family, a promise she made to herself. If the man across from her looks askance at the idea of marriage, there are two ways forward. Either he looks at whether that reluctance is a value or a fear. Or he accepts that the two of them are truly looking in different directions. Both are honest. But persuading someone to settle for a life they do not want makes both of them unhappy.

There is a fine distinction here. Sometimes what we take for a value is really a fear. Being against marriage can be a genuine stance: a deliberate choice about institutions, rules, society's expectations. But it can also be a reflection of unhappy marriages seen in childhood, a fear of being lost, an anxiety about carrying someone for a lifetime. From the outside the two look the same. From the inside they are very different. A value does not change. A fear can soften, with the right person, at the right time. It is easy to say "never" in the abstract. But when there is someone in front of you whom you want to choose again every morning, that "never" sometimes falls silent on its own. And sometimes it does not, and that too tells a person something very clear about themselves.

Then a conclusion follows: someone who does not want marriage can be happy with someone else who does not want marriage. Two people who stand in the same place on this can walk side by side without forcing each other. But the sentence "I don't want marriage" does not mean the same thing for everyone. For one it means "I don't want to be tied down." For another it means "I don't want the institution, but I would want a lifetime with you." For another it means "not yet." So the real question is not marriage, but what kind of life is imagined without it. Two people can say the same word and mean different lives.

Sometimes a person does not need marriage because they are already feeding their needs from other places. There are people around us who travel a lot, make a lot of music, dive deep into a single passion. And at the end of the day they say, "I want to get married, I need someone." Others have spread themselves across many places. They have found their curiosity in different fields, their excitement in different pursuits, their closeness in their friends. In them the voice saying "I need someone" does not sound so loud. The social psychologist Eli Finkel describes one of the troubles of modern relationships like this: today people expect everything from a single person. Lover, best friend, confidant, companion in adventure, mentor. In the past these needs were spread across the village, the family, friends. Now they are loaded onto one pair of shoulders. Someone who spreads their needs across many baskets does not have to place that weight on a single person.

Friendship can truly be enough. It gives closeness, conversation, support. It makes laughing, travelling and sharing together possible. But some things it usually does not fully meet. Physical closeness. Being first in someone's life. Sharing an ordinary Tuesday evening from start to finish. Growing old together. Needing these, or not, depends on the person. Some never do. Some realise it years later.

And here one last fine line remains. Spreading yourself across many places is a strength. But sometimes it can also be a way of protecting yourself from deep attachment. Never needing anything or anyone completely also lowers the risk of loss. From the outside, a choice and an escape look the same. From the inside, a person feels the difference over time. Knowing this makes it possible to decide out of desire rather than out of fear.

In the end values are like a map. Love brings two people to the same point. But the map says where to go. The closer two maps are, the lighter the road. Some differences enrich the road; others divide it. What matters is that no one has to burn their own map. Because love grows not when one person gives themselves up, but when two people, remaining themselves, can look in the same direction.
