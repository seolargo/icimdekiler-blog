baslik: The Unknown Face of Attraction
---
In Turkish the word "çekim" turns up in many places. In physics it is gravity, the force by which the Earth holds us to the ground. In grammar it is conjugation, the way a verb changes form. But perhaps the "çekim" we are most curious about is the one between two people: attraction. Your heart racing when you see someone, being unable to stop thinking about them, looking for excuses to be near them... In love, attraction is this feeling that is hard to explain but that everyone knows.

This feeling is not made of one thing. Sometimes a person's looks, voice or smile draw us. That is physical attraction. Sometimes we feel at ease talking with them, we feel understood. That is emotional attraction. And sometimes their way of thinking, the questions they ask, occupy our mind. That is intellectual attraction. The strongest bonds are usually born where these three meet.

There is a little chemistry involved too. In the first days of attraction the brain releases dopamine and gives us a sense of reward. Noradrenaline speeds the heart and keeps us from sleeping. Serotonin drops and that person will not leave our mind. So the saying "they made me lose my mind" is, in part, true.

But attraction does not always take us somewhere good. Sometimes we are drawn to the wrong person. Knowingly, again and again.

The first reason is familiarity. As children we take the love we saw to be "normal." Someone who grew up with a distant parent may, as an adult, turn toward distant people too. Such people do not feel wrong to them; they feel like home. Another reason is uncertainty. Someone who is attentive one day and cold the next works on the brain like gambling. When it is unclear when the reward will come, the excitement grows. And we mistake that anxiety for love. Someone calm and reliable, by comparison, seems "boring."

Sometimes another question lies beneath attraction: "Am I worth something?" Someone who does not feel good enough may choose someone cold and difficult. What runs through them is: "If this person who is closed to everyone opens up to me, it means I am special." The other person becomes a test. Winning them becomes a way of telling oneself "I am enough." Yet the difficult person usually does not change. And because giving up feels like saying "I am not enough," the person stays where they are unhappy.

Among these reasons perhaps the most familiar is the wish to rescue. Some of us are drawn to troubled, wounded, "broken" people. We want to help them, to fix them. Because being good for someone gives us meaning. Being needed, being useful, seems the easiest way to feel valuable. This tendency often goes back a long way. Those who as children had to look after someone at home, calm things down, manage the feelings of the adults, go on looking for the same role when they grow up.

But here there is a bitter truth: a person changes only if they themselves want to. However hard we try, we cannot walk someone else's road in their place. The relationship we try to rescue loses its equality over time and turns into a patient and carer arrangement. The more we give, the more tired we become. And when we want to leave, we feel guilty. Yet helping and rescuing are not the same thing. Helping is supporting someone as they carry their own load. Rescuing is shouldering that load in their place.

So what should we do? Perhaps the first step is to ask ourselves an honest question: "Am I drawn to this person, or to the idea of fixing them?" The second step is not to mistake intense excitement for compatibility. The third is to know people not by their words but by their behaviour over time. And perhaps the hardest: to learn to find ourselves valuable not only when we are useful, but as we are.

A healthy relationship can feel unexciting at first. The heart does not beat so fast, sleep does not slip away. But this is not dullness; it is calm. Perhaps that is what real attraction is: a force that does not fling us about but settles us in our place. Just as the Earth holds us to the ground.
