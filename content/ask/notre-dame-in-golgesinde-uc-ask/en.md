---
In the Paris of 1482, a young woman dances in the square before Notre-Dame. Her name is Esmeralda. A white goat walks beside her and she carries a tambourine. When Victor Hugo wrote the novel in 1831, he also wanted to save the Gothic cathedral that was being left to decay. Yet what readers remembered was not the stone but the three men who looked at the same woman from its shadow.

All three love Esmeralda. What each of them loves is very different.

## Frollo: to possess

Claude Frollo is the archdeacon of the cathedral. He is learned and disciplined, a man who has given his life to books and faith. The moment he sees Esmeralda, all of that order collapses.

What Frollo feels is less love than defeat. A man who has repressed himself for years blames her for the one thing he cannot repress. In the end he offers her two choices: himself or the gallows. It is the darkest moment of the novel, and Frollo carries the choice through to the end. With his own hands he brings the woman he claims to love closer to the gallows.

Here love means seeing the other person not as a human being but as a problem that disturbs one's own peace.

## Phoebus: to consume

Captain Phoebus is handsome, easygoing and shallow. He rescues Esmeralda one night and she gives him her whole heart. For Phoebus it is a small adventure on the side of his engagement.

When Esmeralda is condemned to death, Phoebus is alive and knows it. He says nothing. His affection lasts only as long as it costs him nothing.

One of the most painful things in the novel is that Esmeralda loves, to the very end, the man who deserves it least. Love sometimes chooses not the right person but the one who shines brightest.

## Quasimodo: to keep alive

Quasimodo is the bell-ringer of the cathedral. He is hunchbacked, has one eye, and the noise of the bells has made him deaf. Paris sees him as a monster.

His real meeting with Esmeralda happens at the pillory. Quasimodo has been flogged and is dying of thirst under the sun. The crowd laughs. Only Esmeralda steps out of it and gives him water.

Quasimodo never forgets that single kindness. When she is led to the gallows he takes her in his arms, carries her into the cathedral and shouts "Sanctuary!" He hides her there, feeds her and protects her. He stays away so that she will not have to see his face. He asks for nothing in return. He knows she loves Phoebus, and still he tries to bring Phoebus to her.

Of the three loves Hugo describes, the only true one is the love that gains its owner nothing.

## The last scene, in stone

The novel does not end well. Esmeralda is hanged. Quasimodo pushes Frollo, who is laughing as he watches from the cathedral tower, over the edge. Then he looks at the two dead, the woman on the gallows and the man on the pavement, and says, "There is everything I ever loved!" Then he disappears.

Years later two skeletons are found in the mass grave at Montfaucon. One belongs to a woman, the other to a man with a twisted spine. The man's bones are wrapped around the woman's. When they try to separate them, the skeleton crumbles into dust.

## Seen from today

Hugo's three men read like three definitions of love. Frollo's is mistaking the beloved for a cure for one's own lack. Phoebus's is living love as entertainment. Quasimodo's is putting the beloved's happiness before one's own.

The question that stays after the last page is this: when we say we love someone, which of the three do we mean? Most of us believe we love like Quasimodo. Looking honestly, we find a little Frollo and a little Phoebus in ourselves too.

Perhaps what makes love mature is being able to tell these three apart within us.
