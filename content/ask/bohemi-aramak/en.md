---
baslik: Looking for Bohème
---
Bohème is always told in the past tense. Aznavour's song begins that way too: I am going to tell you about a time that those under twenty cannot know. The painter, recalling his youth in Montmartre, builds every sentence in the past. We were happy. We were hungry. We were young. We were mad. Bohème is something that even those who lived it can only name afterwards. Perhaps that is why everyone who looks for it feels a little too late.

Why are people drawn to bohème? Perhaps because every corner of modern life looks alike. The same shops, the same windows, the same hurry. Everything has a price, a plan, an outcome. Bohème promises something else: a place where time is plentiful, money seems unimportant, and people call one another by name. Conversations that last all night, half-finished paintings, poems read around a stove. Looking at it, you feel that something has been left missing in your own life.

But looking for bohème is a strange business. Because the more you look, the more it slips away.

When a neighbourhood becomes bohemian, at first a few people notice. Then more. Then a magazine writes about it, a list is published, photographs are shared. People come, cafés open, rents rise. And the people who made the place bohemian can no longer afford to live there. That is what happened to Cihangir. Then Moda, then Yeldeğirmeni. Each time the bohemian spirit withdrew a little farther, a little closer to the edge. Now it lives scattered in small villages on the Aegean, on the slopes of the Kaz Mountains, in the cities of other countries.

Bohème is like a candle. Its light draws people in, but the more of them gather around it, the faster it melts. Most of those who look for it are, without realising, among those who bring about its end.

There is also this: most people drawn to bohème are really drawn to its image. Sitting in a café for hours, living in an old building, walking the same street as artists. But bohemian life has another side too. Eating every other day. Cold studios. Never being known. Having no security at all about the future. Today no one knows the names of most of the painters of Montmartre. They were part of a generation, but they vanished inside it. People want the poetry of bohème, not its poverty. Yet that poetry was born largely out of that poverty.

So is looking for bohème pointless?

Perhaps we are looking in the wrong place. We look for bohème in a neighbourhood, a city, an address. But what made Montmartre bohemian was neither its lilacs nor its steep streets. It was a handful of people gathered in cheap rooms, in the same cafés, around the same stove. And those people shared a condition: they were young, they had time, and they believed in making something. Bohème was not a place but a time. An era. A state of being.

When Aznavour's painter returns to his old address years later, he recognises nothing. Not the walls, not the streets. His studio is gone. The lilacs have died. But perhaps it was not only Montmartre that had changed. The painter was no longer twenty either. One of the things that had made that neighbourhood bohemian was his own youth. And youth cannot be found again at any address.

That is why someone looking for bohème is perhaps looking not for a place but for a state of being. A state where time is plentiful, where making something feels meaningful, where people are close to one another. And this state, unfortunately, can neither be rented through an advertisement nor chosen from a list.

But perhaps it can be built.

Finding bohème is hard, but remaking it on a small scale may be possible. A few friends who meet on the same evening every week. People who show each other their unfinished work. A cheap table, a long conversation, something made without having to prove anything to anyone. A small community, unpretentious, unnamed, that will never make it onto any list. Perhaps that is the only way bohème can live: as long as no one notices it.

And perhaps one day, years later, someone describing those days will begin like this: I am going to tell you about a time you cannot know.

That is the moment you understand that bohème was truly lived. Because bohème is always noticed afterwards.
