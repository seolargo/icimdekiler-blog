---
baslik: Looking for Bohème
---
Bohème is always told in the past tense. Aznavour's song begins that way too: I am going to tell you about a time that those under twenty cannot know. The painter, recalling his youth in Montmartre, builds every sentence in the past. We were happy. We were hungry. We were young. We were mad. Bohème is something that even those who lived it can only name afterwards. Perhaps that is why everyone who looks for it feels a little too late.

Why are people drawn to bohème? Perhaps because every corner of modern life looks alike. The same shops, the same windows, the same hurry. Everything has a price, a plan, an outcome. Bohème promises something else: a place where time is plentiful, money seems unimportant, and people call one another by name. Conversations that last all night, half-finished paintings, poems read around a stove. Looking at it, you feel that something has been left missing in your own life.

But looking for bohème is a strange business. Because the more you look, the more it slips away.

When a neighbourhood becomes bohemian, at first a few people notice. Then more. Then a magazine writes about it, a list is published, photographs are shared. People come, cafés open, rents rise. And the people who made the place bohemian can no longer afford to live there. That is what happened to Cihangir. Then Moda, then Yeldeğirmeni. Each time the bohemian spirit withdrew a little farther, a little closer to the edge. Now it lives scattered in small villages on the Aegean, on the slopes of the Kaz Mountains, in the cities of other countries.

Bohème is like a candle. Its light draws people in, but the more of them gather around it, the faster it melts. Most of those who look for it are, without realising, among those who bring about its end.

There is also this: most people drawn to bohème are really drawn to its image. Sitting in a café for hours, living in an old building, walking the same street as artists. But bohemian life has another side too. Eating every other day. Cold studios. Never being known. Having no security at all about the future. Today no one knows the names of most of the painters of Montmartre. They were part of a generation, but they vanished inside it. People want the poetry of bohème, not its poverty. Yet that poetry was born largely out of that poverty.

So is looking for bohème pointless?

Perhaps we are looking in the wrong place. We look for bohème in a neighbourhood, a city, an address. But what made Montmartre bohemian was neither its lilacs nor its steep streets. It was a handful of people gathered in cheap rooms, in the same cafés, around the same stove. And those people shared a condition: they were young, they had time, and they believed in making something. Bohème was not a place but a time. An era. A state of being.

When Aznavour's painter returns to his old address years later, he recognises nothing. Not the walls, not the streets. His studio is gone. The lilacs have died. But perhaps it was not only Montmartre that had changed. The painter was no longer twenty either. One of the things that had made that neighbourhood bohemian was his own youth. And youth cannot be found again at any address.

That is why someone looking for bohème is perhaps looking not for a place but for a state of being. A state where time is plentiful, where making something feels meaningful, where people are close to one another.

One might object: societies did not progress through bohème. Societies progressed through science and engineering. That is largely true. Living longer, eating better, travelling faster; vaccines, electricity, railways and computers came not out of bohemian cafés but out of laboratories, factories and workshops.

But the relationship between the two is more entangled than it seems. Some of science's great breakthroughs were born in places very like bohemian communities. In the Vienna of the 1920s, philosophers and scientists gathered in cafés to argue about the foundations of science; this would later be called the "Vienna Circle." At Bell Labs, researchers were given the freedom to work for long stretches without a particular result being expected; both the transistor and information theory came out of such an environment. The early days of Silicon Valley were intertwined with the counterculture of the 1960s. Some of those who built the first personal computers were young people who gathered in garages and hobby clubs and gave little thought to money. The conditions that nourish progress are surprisingly similar to those that nourish bohème: time, freedom, a small community and the right to fail.

Bohemian communities do not usually produce machines, but they produce new ways of seeing. Modern art, modern architecture and design, a large part of the visual language we use today came out of such settings. The artists of the Bauhaus shaped today's spare product design and even the look of the interfaces we see on screens. Bohemians also pushed society's limits: on who could live how, and on what could be talked about.

There is also a question of order. Bohemian culture is often a result of progress. Montmartre could appear only in an industrialised, prosperous Paris. A society cannot allow some of its members to live without caring about money until it produces enough. So engineering makes bohème possible. The engine that carries society is science and engineering; bohème is not the engine but a testing ground. Yet a good engineering culture also often needs something bohemian: a space that permits curiosity, lingering and working on things that are of no immediate use.

Perhaps what everyone needs is not bohemian life itself but something that bohemian neighbourhoods provide. The sociologist Ray Oldenburg has a term for it: the "third place." The first place is home, the second is work. The third place is outside both, where people gather informally: the coffeehouse, the café, the tavern, the barber's, the neighbourhood park, the bookshop. For Oldenburg these places are vital to the health of a society, because there people can simply be together without having to prove anything to anyone.

Bohemian neighbourhoods are the most concentrated form of these third places. But they hold something more: an atmosphere that permits making, wondering and lingering. A space where a person can say, "Today I did something that was of no use at all, and it did me good." An ordinary third place rests a person; a creative third place sets them in motion. Places where people do not only consume but also bring something; where a half-finished idea can be put on the table freely; where an engineer and a painter, a writer and a student can meet at the same table.

Not everyone wants to be bohemian. But most people need, from time to time, to stop by a place where they see the same faces, can sit without hurrying, meet different people, and where thinking and making are treated as normal. As cities lose these places, people are squeezed into a lonely life shuttling between home and work. Part of today's sense of loneliness comes from here.

Some cities have noticed this. In the early 2000s Amsterdam saw that its artists were leaving the city. In response it started a programme that gave old factories, warehouses and school buildings to artists and creative people as cheap studios and living space. These places are called "broedplaats," meaning a breeding ground. The municipality keeps the rents low on purpose. Because it knows that if they were left to the market, the rents would destroy these places. Bohème is born on its own, but it cannot live on its own. Protecting it takes someone's deliberate decision.

And this state can neither be rented through an advertisement nor chosen from a list. But perhaps it can be built.

Finding bohème is hard, but remaking it on a small scale may be possible. A few friends who meet on the same evening every week. People who show each other their unfinished work. A cheap table, a long conversation, something made without having to prove anything to anyone. A small community, unpretentious, unnamed, that will never make it onto any list. Perhaps that is the only way bohème can live: as long as no one notices it.

And perhaps one day, years later, someone describing those days will begin like this: I am going to tell you about a time you cannot know.

That is the moment you understand that bohème was truly lived. Because bohème is always noticed afterwards.
