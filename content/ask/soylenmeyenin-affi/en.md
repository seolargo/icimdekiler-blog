baslik: Forgiving What Was Never Said
---
We usually tie forgiveness to an event. Someone said something cruel to us, broke a promise, betrayed us. There is a wound with a date, a place and someone who did it. Forgiving means placing a decision over that wound: "You did this, but I will no longer call you to account."

Yet some of the things that wound us most are things that never happened. An "I love you" never said, a "well done" that never came, a "thank you" that was never quite spoken. These have no date. No one knows on which day they went missing. One morning you wake up and realise you have been waiting for something for years, and it never came.

What makes these wounds hard is that there is nothing to blame. No one shouted at you. No one hit you. There was food on the table, the lights were on at home, the school fees were paid. Someone looking from outside would see nothing missing. Sometimes you cannot see it either. All that remains is a hunger inside you that has no name. Cruelty at least leaves a mark. Absence does not even leave a mark; it leaves only an empty space.

The most insidious thing about that empty space is that it keeps the waiting alive. A wrong that was done stays in the past. But a word never spoken looks as if it could still be spoken. Maybe tomorrow. Maybe on a holiday morning. Maybe in a hospital, at one last moment. You live with that possibility. And as you live, you start to see the other person as a debtor. With every glance, at every silent dinner, a quiet ledger is kept: "You still owe me this."

Forgiving a wound like this sounds strange. Because in our minds forgiveness has a fixed shape. First the other side apologises, then they are forgiven. Forgiving is taken to mean approving what was done, or saying "it doesn't matter." It also looks a little like a gesture from above, like the "amnesty" with which a state lifts a sentence. That is why saying "I forgive you" is so often hard. It feels as if we are handing the other side a reward they have not earned. All the more so when there has not even been an apology.

But when we look at where the word comes from, something else appears. The Turkish "af" comes from the Arabic root "afv," and its original meaning is to erase. In old Arabic the same root is used for the wind wiping footprints from the sand. In ancient Greek, the word the Gospels use for forgiving means "to let go, to release." The same word is used for cancelling a debt; the prayer "forgive us our debts" comes from it. The French "pardonner," the Spanish "perdonar" and the English "forgive" point in another direction: to give completely, to give without return. The Turkish "bağışlamak," to grant or to spare, carries the same idea.

So the word itself speaks neither of approval nor of reward. It speaks of erasing an account, of putting down a burden, of giving without expecting anything back. And none of these requires the other side to apologise.

Forgiving what was never said is close to exactly this old meaning. This forgiveness does not acquit the other person. Nor does it tell them "there's no problem." There was a problem. The absence was real. Forgiving is only saying: "I will no longer try to collect this debt from you."

This may look like surrender. But it is in fact a release. Because the person who keeps the ledger is as much a prisoner as a creditor. Every day they look at the same door and wait for the same voice. Their life turns around a gesture someone else never made. The person who closes the ledger can, for the first time, lift their head and look in another direction.

And then something interesting happens. What was never given begins to be looked for elsewhere. In a teacher's sincere praise, in a friend's unexpected embrace, in a "you did well" a person learns to say to themselves. These do not come from where they were first expected. Perhaps none of them fully fills that first absence. But the person no longer waits in front of the door. They set out on their own.

This forgiveness has nothing to do with forgetting. The wind erases the footprint in the sand, but the road that was walked has been walked. What is erased is not the memory but the account. Like that line in a song: "I forgive you, but I remember everything." A sentence that looks at first like a contradiction is in fact forgiveness itself. Words never spoken go on being remembered. Perhaps one day, saying "I love you" to your own child, you will understand why your voice trembles a little. Remembering does not keep the pain alive. What keeps the pain alive is waiting.

Perhaps this is the hardest forgiveness of all: forgiving not a wrong but an absence. Because when you forgive a wrong, you make peace with the past. When you forgive an absence, you give up a possibility. You accept that what you most wanted to come will not come. And only then is room made for everything that might come in its place.
