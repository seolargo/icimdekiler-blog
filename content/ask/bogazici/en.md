---
baslik: Boğaziçi
---
Some dreams always stand in beautiful light in a person's mind. Going out with a girl who studies at Boğaziçi. Walking among the trees of the campus, talking for hours in a café in Bebek. Then going to Germany together, building a life there from scratch. Going on holiday together in the summers, seeing new cities, new shores. And perhaps, deeper than all of it, witnessing a woman's earliest youth. Standing at the very beginning of her life. Being there for her first excitements, her first fears, her first mistakes.

At first sight this last wish looks like an innocent longing. But there is anger in it too. Because those years were lived; you simply were not there. Others saw. Others touched. In her youth, perhaps in her heart, someone was there before you. And that someone was most likely not a passer-by. At the time he mattered to her. That is the thought that hurts. Psychology calls this retroactive jealousy. Anger and jealousy toward the life the person you love had before you. The question "why wasn't it me" mixed with anger.

The roots of this anger run deep. For years culture described virginity as a gift, as a man's right. The first, it was said, is unforgettable. Even if a person does not believe this with their mind, somewhere inside the message remains. There is also the fear of being compared. The thought "Is she comparing me with him?" And at the bottom, perhaps the strongest of all, the wish to possess completely. That no part of the person you love should belong to anyone else.

The consolations offered about this rarely help. "You can't be her first, but you can be her last," people say. But talking about the future to someone whose trouble is the past is like telling a hungry person they will eat tomorrow. To be honest, there is no way to satisfy this wish. The past does not change. And part of the anger perhaps comes from exactly this. Wanting something very much and knowing it will never be possible. It is a kind of mourning. It does not pass by saying "all right, I understand." It only becomes bearable with time.

At this point a question comes to mind: if I cannot be her first, then let her not be my last. Like a good trade. But this is not a trade. No one gains anything. Her past does not change, and your anger does not pass. Besides, the scales are already balanced. You have a past too. You are not her first, and she is not yours. If you want to let go, you should let go because you do not want it. Not to get even.

So what exactly is this feeling? Perhaps it is not only jealousy. There is a word in German: Sehnsucht. A deep longing for something not quite known, something that may never have existed. The writer C. S. Lewis described this feeling throughout his life. For him, a person always attaches this longing to something. To a person, a place, a memory. But reaching it, one is never fully satisfied. Because the true object of the longing is nothing in this world.

This longing has other relatives in other languages. In Portuguese, saudade: a yearning for something once had and now gone, knowing it will not return. In Welsh, hiraeth: longing for a home one cannot return to, a home that perhaps never existed. In German, fernweh: a yearning for faraway places never visited. And a recently coined word, anemoia: nostalgia for a time one never lived. The sorrow of not having witnessed a woman's youth fits exactly inside this word.

The search for virginity is perhaps a Sehnsucht too. Beneath it lies a longing to return to the beginning of something, to reach an untouched start, to belong completely. And this longing has taken the shape of virginity through culture and jealousy. This has an important consequence. If it really is Sehnsucht, then even with a woman whose first you were, the feeling would not fully settle. Because that is the nature of this longing. Even when you reach it, you say, "this wasn't it."

The other scenes are the same. The girl studying at Boğaziçi, the life in Germany, the holidays. All of them stand in the mind in their polished versions. Without friction, without dull moments, like photographs always taken from the right angle. That is why Sehnsucht tastes sweet. But in reality the same scenes have a bitter side. That girl gets tired too, gets angry, struggles with her own troubles. She is tense in exam week, she has problems with her family. In Germany there are visa matters, the scramble to find a flat, the language, loneliness and grey winter days. On holiday the plane is delayed, there are fights, the money runs short. Living in reality is hard.

It is a bit like eating a moderately spicy dish. The food is sweet, but it burns too. And people eat it precisely for that reason. If it were only sweet, they would tire of it. If it were only hot, no one would eat it. The taste of real life is like that. Both come together.

But one thing needs care. Most of these scenes are not impossible. Meeting someone who studies at Boğaziçi, building a life in Germany, setting out on the road together are things that can truly be lived. They are simply lived differently from how they look in the mind. Virginity is another matter. It looks toward something left in the past that cannot be brought back. That is why it hurts the most.

So what should one do with this longing? There are two ways. Either you use it as a yardstick, or as a compass. If you use it as a yardstick, real life always tastes bitter by comparison. No woman, no city, no holiday can match that polished photograph in the mind. Everything falls short. If you use it as a compass, it shows you a direction. It tells you where you want to go, but not what it will be like when you get there.

The first step in turning a dream into a compass is to find the plain wish beneath it. Beneath a girl who studies at Boğaziçi lies the wish for someone clever and curious, someone who can talk with you at the level of ideas. Beneath Germany, the wish for a new life, for change, for building something together. Beneath the holiday, the wish for shared adventure, for seeing new things together. And beneath virginity, the wish to be at the beginning of something, to belong completely, to be the only one. Wishes are more flexible than scenes. Someone clever and curious can be at Boğaziçi, or somewhere else. A new life can be built in Germany, or in another country.

The second step is to separate the polish from the scene. That perfect light in the dream, that smoothness, is the polish. Saying yes to the wish means saying no to the polish. Because in reality that wish will come together with tiredness, with fights, with dull days.

The third step is to take a small and real step. A compass shows the direction, but you have to walk. If the wish is Germany, a language course, a few job listings, going once to see it. If the wish is for a partner in thought, entering the places where such people are found. If the wish is adventure, first a journey alone.

The fourth step is not to compare with the dream once you arrive. When you live something, you will say, "it's not like in my dream." That is normal. The real question is not the polish but the substance: was my wish met?

And finally, updating the dream. As you live, the dream changes too. Once you go to Germany you will want something else. Being with someone, you will learn what really matters. A compass is not fixed; it recalibrates as you walk.

Returning to virginity, the only way to turn it into a compass is to take the wish out of the past and carry it into the present. You cannot be at the beginning of a woman's life. But you can build a bond with someone in which you belong completely, in which you truly open up to each other. Not in the past, but now. This does not take away all the anger. But at least it turns it from something that cannot be changed toward something that can be done.

While thinking about all this, one thing still stays in place: the idea of marriage. Whatever happens, it feels flawed. Perhaps because of the marriages you have seen, perhaps from the fear of losing your freedom, perhaps because you know that no one can transform anyone's life. Loving does not promise meadows. When someone enters your life, your life does not turn into a flower garden. There is simply someone beside you. And for some that is enough, for some it is not. That too is a perfectly valid stance. Marriage is not right for everyone. Many people live full and meaningful lives without ever marrying. Not wanting marriage does not mean not wanting to love, to let someone into your life, or to form a bond. It only means knowing that the institution is not for you. When entering a relationship, it is enough to say this clearly from the start. That way no one enters your life with the wrong expectation, and you have not forced anyone onto a road they did not want.

In the end Boğaziçi remains a campus that always stands in the same light in the mind. Perhaps you will never walk on that campus with someone. Perhaps you will, and it will not be like your dream. But the dream is telling you something. Where you want to look. Whom you want to talk with. What kind of life you are after. Living the dream is not possible. But walking toward it is. With its sweetness and its sting.
