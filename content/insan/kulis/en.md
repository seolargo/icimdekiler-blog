baslik: Backstage
---
Think of a ballerina. She stands on the tips of her toes in the middle of the stage. Arms raised, face calm. As if gravity had forgotten her. The audience holds its breath, and none of them knows in that moment how much her feet hurt. Her bleeding toes, her bandaged ankles, the same movement repeated for years. The whole art of ballet lies here: making the hardest thing look like the easiest. Hiding the pain. Looking light.

Then the curtain falls. The ballerina goes backstage. She takes off her shoes, sits on the floor, perhaps leans against the wall for a while without a word. There she is no longer a swan. She is a tired woman. And who is allowed into that backstage says more than what happens on stage.

I think most of us are a little like ballerinas in life. On stage we stand upright. At work we play the strong one. With friends, the easygoing one. At home, the one who carries the responsibility. Everywhere we are a little careful, a little controlled. We adjust our faces, we weigh our voices. We show no one our struggle. No one asks anyway. The applause is always for the stage.

The circus speaks differently. There the acrobat can fall. And everyone knows it. The rope sways, the trapeze reaches out into empty air, the audience shuts its eyes for a moment. The beauty of the circus lies exactly in this risk. Ballet says, "look how easy it is." The circus says, "look how dangerous it is, and I'm doing it anyway." One hides the pain; the other puts the possibility of falling at the centre of the stage.

Some shows turn this into a story. A lonely, withdrawn child steps through a door a clown has opened into a strange, funny world. A clown watches his own funeral and sees his whole life again, and understands that even death can be a dance. On a stage full of water, people dive and surface and let themselves go with the flow. The same feeling circulates through all those scenes: beginning alone, fearing the fall, and still one day rising into the air among others.

Perhaps what we most want from someone lies somewhere between these two.

We know how to stand upright on stage. We have done it for years. But a wish grows inside us: someone before whom we can be weak. Someone we can let backstage. Someone with whom we can stay tired when we are tired, frightened when we are afraid, silly when we are being silly. Someone who does not turn their face away when we take off our shoes and sit on the floor and they see our bleeding toes. And who, having seen it, goes on sitting beside us.

This is a very human wish. Perhaps the most precious thing we can ask of someone.

But the backstage door does not open all at once. No one lets everyone in after the first show. First you show something small. A tiredness, a worry, an old fear. Then you look at the other person's face. Did they listen? Did they laugh? Did they look away? If they listened, you open the door a little wider. That is how trust builds up. Like thin sheets of paper, layer upon layer. Each one is very light. But over time they become solid ground.

And this is not a one-person show. You must be able to go into their backstage, and they into yours. If one is taking off their shoes while the other is still posing on stage, there is no intimacy there. Intimacy is two people being a little defenceless at the same time. A night when both of them wipe off their make-up.

There is a risk in this, I know. Like the acrobat in the circus. When you show yourself, you can fall. Someone may look at your wound and wince. That fear is real. But no one ever learned to fly on a trapeze without a net either. The acrobat who never jumps does not fall, true. But neither does he ever fly.

And there is this. The person who can come into your backstage is the person who does not expect you to be flawless. There are things you have hidden for years. What you do not know, what you have not lived, the places where you feel you are late. You have put them in a chest behind the stage and locked it. Beside the right person, that chest opens one day. And you are surprised. What comes out does not drive them away. Your inexperience, your past, your shortcomings. They are all spread out on the floor, and they are still there. They look at them and know that these too are you. They stop being things to hide. They become the pieces that make you who you are.

Perhaps on stage we always have to look light. But everyone deserves a backstage where they can take off their shoes and sit on the floor, and someone who quietly comes into that backstage and sits down beside them.
