baslik: On Narcissism
---
In everyday speech the word "narcissist" is used very often. We readily attach this label to anyone who behaves selfishly, hurts us or ends our relationship. Yet narcissism is not one single thing; it is a spectrum with different degrees and different faces. At one end stands healthy self-esteem, in the middle narcissistic traits, and at the far end narcissistic personality disorder.

At the core of narcissism lie a few shared features. The person sees themselves as special and superior, constantly seeks admiration and approval, and believes they should be granted privileges. They either fail to notice others' feelings or do not care about them. They may use people for a purpose, overreact to criticism, envy others or believe that others envy them. The common source of these features is not, as is often thought, a strong self-confidence, but on the contrary a fragile sense of self-worth. This shaky inner sense of worth constantly needs to be fed from outside.

There are two basic ways of protecting this fragility, and the two main types of narcissism come from them. The overt or grandiose narcissist announces their worth loudly to the outside world. They are self-assured, showy and dominant; they like to be the centre of attention and can seem charismatic from the outside. The covert or vulnerable narcissist carries the same belief, "I am special," inside but cannot demand it openly. They fear rejection and humiliation. So their sense of superiority stays silent, and shows outwardly as hurt, resentment and victimhood.

Although the workings of covert narcissism look contradictory at first, the logic is simple. Deep down the person thinks they deserve more, but does not let it show. And the world, naturally, does not treat them as someone special. The result is the feeling "no one understands me, they don't know my worth." In other words, this resentment is the result of an unmet expectation of superiority.

Three behaviours stand out in this type. The first is a strong fantasy world. A person who receives no recognition in real life builds scenes in their mind in which everyone finally sees their worth. But these fantasies do not turn into action, because a real attempt carries the risk of failure; in fantasy one always wins. Here the fantasy serves not as a goal but as a consolation. The second is being modest on the outside and superior on the inside. Someone who openly praises themselves becomes open to criticism; the covert narcissist does not take that risk and stays in the background. But inwardly they keep comparing and find those around them shallow or worthless. The modesty here is not real humility but a way of hiding. The third is indifference to other people's problems, and the conversation always turning back to themselves. The reason is often not malice but that their attention is constantly on their own wound. Another person's pain reminds them of their own, and the conversation soon slides toward the injustices they have suffered.

Narcissism also shows some differences in how it appears across the sexes. Research shows that, on average, men score slightly higher on narcissism scales. The difference appears most in the dimensions of entitlement and the desire for authority; in the vulnerable type there is no clear difference between the sexes. In men, narcissism is expressed more through status, money, power and competition. In women, expecting recognition through appearance, social image, making others feel guilty within a relationship, or a self-sacrificing image is more common. But these are only tendencies, and there is a great deal of overlap between individuals.

In relationships, narcissism usually follows a familiar cycle. At first comes a period of idealisation, with intense attention, compliments and rapid attachment. Then comes devaluation, with criticism, belittling, silent punishment and the distortion of reality. In the final stage there is cooling, withdrawal, and sometimes a renewed approach when they need something.

In weighing all of this, the most important distinction is the difference between having narcissistic traits and having narcissistic personality disorder. Almost everyone has narcissistic traits to some degree. These usually depend on the situation, appear in stressful or hurt moments, and then pass. The person can step back when needed, apologise, see their mistake. Their empathy may weaken at times but still works, and they can generally carry on with their life.

Narcissistic personality disorder, by contrast, is a rare condition defined in psychiatry, and only a specialist can diagnose it. Here the pattern lasts for years from young adulthood onward and appears the same way in every area of life, such as work, family, friendship and relationships. The person is very rigid, almost always sees the problem in others, and has great difficulty changing. Most importantly, the condition seriously harms the lives of both the person and those around them. A diagnosis requires at least five of nine criteria to be present over a long period and consistently.

In conclusion, someone showing a few narcissistic behaviours does not mean they are a narcissist or have a disorder. Daydreaming, being hurt from time to time, focusing on one's own troubles in a hard period are ordinary human states. What is decisive is whether these behaviours are constant, whether they repeat in every setting, whether they are closed to change, and whether they cause serious harm. The answers to these questions are the points to consider carefully before attaching a label.
